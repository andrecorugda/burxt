# Burxt — A4.7 Signature Grammar Specification

## AMENDMENTS (binding — decided 2026-07-25, after the v0.0.16 audit)

These two decisions were surfaced by the audit in `spec/README.md` and settled
before implementation. They are **hard constraints on A4.7**, not suggestions,
and they override anything below that conflicts with them.

### Amendment 1 — the brace rule, and the general law behind it

**A bare `{` in a string literal is a COMPILE ERROR demanding `\{`.**
Interpolation is `{expr}`; literal braces are `\{` and `\}`.

The alternative — letting `{` simply start meaning interpolation — was rejected
on principle, not convenience: `print("hi {name}")` compiles today and prints
the braces literally, so reinterpreting it would silently change the meaning of
existing valid programs. That is the exact sin Burxt refuses everywhere else,
and a language that silently reinterprets valid syntax once will do it again.

This generalizes into a law, and it belongs in the design record:

> **A feature that changes what currently-valid syntax MEANS must make the old
> form a compile error, never silently reinterpret it.** The breaking error,
> pointing at the exact fix, is the feature working correctly — not a cost of
> shipping it.

Implementation constraint: brace handling belongs in the **lexer's string
scanner**, so `{`, `}`, `\{`, `\}` are all resolved at tokenization and the
parser has no ambiguity left to settle.

### Amendment 2 — no type inference in this milestone

`let price = $19.99` would require inferring a binding's type from its
initializer. **No `let` in Burxt does that today, and A4.7 must not be the
milestone that introduces it.**

So the money, percent and unit literals ship as **values that still require
annotations**:

```burxt
let price: Decimal<2> = $19.99;              // fine
let price = $19.99;                          // still a compile error
```

This delivers the entire demo — `let total: Decimal<2> = price + price * 8.25%;`
reads exactly as intended — without smuggling a type-system change in through a
dollar sign.

**Why this is separated rather than bundled:** how much inference a language
has is one of the largest levers on its whole ergonomic character — the
easy↔safe axis the North Star names as the permanent tension. `let x = 5`
versus `let x: Int = 5`, across every line of every program, is a large part of
how a language *feels*. A decision that shapes the feel of every line must be
made on its own, with its own spec, its own reach boundary, and its own
must-NOT list (starting with: no inference across function signatures — those
stay explicit, so inference never becomes action at a distance). It must not
arrive as an implementation detail of a literal form.

**Local type inference is therefore its own milestone**, and a strong candidate
for the one right after A4.7, since it is the single largest ergonomic win
available and is low-risk once bounded. Its options, for that spec to decide:
(1) no inference, (2) literal-only inference, (3) full local inference within a
function body. Note it adds **no** M1 ledger entry — it is a pure front-end
question, not an ownership-blocked one.


> Status: **spec, to implement.** This is the "eloquence" milestone — the 5% novel
> grammar where Burxt's thesis lives. Judgment-heavy like the object-model phases,
> so §5 (must NOT do) is load-bearing. Governing rule: **95% familiar, 5% novel
> exactly where the thesis lives.** Every addition must read like the intent, not
> like a clever symbol.

### Amendment 3 — mixed-scale multiplication (SETTLED; supersedes the open question below)

**`*` may take operands of DIFFERENT scales, but only when the result binding
carries a rounding contract. `+` and `-` remain strict.**

The asymmetry is principled, not a compromise, and it states in one sentence:

> **Addition combines like quantities, so scales must match. Multiplication
> combines a quantity with a rate, so scales differ by nature.**

`$1.00 + $0.001` is almost always a bug — a units or precision mistake —
because addition means "these are the same kind of thing, combine them," and
differing scales suggest they are not. But money × rate is inherently an
operation between two *different* kinds of quantity: a price is scale-2, a tax
or interest or exchange rate is naturally more precise. Forcing those to match
is forcing a fiction.

**The rounding contract is the load-bearing safety requirement, and it is
mandatory.** A mixed-scale product's natural scale is the sum of the operand
scales, so landing it on the declared scale loses digits — and someone must
say how. That is exactly what contracts exist for:

```burxt
let total: Decimal<2, RoundHalfEven> = price + price * 8.25%;
```

The `*` lands at scale 2 via the contract; then `+` sees matching scales.
Exact, no float, no silent rounding.

Without a contract on the result, mixed-scale `*` is a compile error naming
the fix. **This must never become optional** — if mixed scales can be
multiplied into a silently-rounded result, the thesis is broken; if the
rounding must be declared, it is preserved.

**Why this is more on-thesis than staying strict.** The strict form already
"works": widen the money to the rate's scale by hand, multiply, narrow back.
But that is the *author* performing rescaling and rounding manually, which is
more error-prone and less readable than declaring a contract and letting the
compiler do it exactly. Strictness here does not prevent the precision loss —
it just makes a human perform it. Burxt exists so the compiler handles
exactness; forcing manual rescaling for the most common money operation works
against that.

The rule, complete:

| Operation | Scales | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| `+`, `-` | must match | unchanged |
| `*` | same | legal; contract required as before (the scale still doubles) |
| `*` | mixed | legal **only** with a rounding contract on the result, which governs how the natural sum-of-scales product narrows |
| `/` | must match | unchanged (this decision covers `*` only) |

Intermediates compute in i128 (v0.0.11), so the exact product is available
before rounding.

## ~~OPEN DECISION~~ — resolved by Amendment 3 above

*Kept for the reasoning trail; the question below is answered by Amendment 3.*

**The spec's flagship percent example does not compile, and making it compile
requires changing a core arithmetic rule.**

§1b promises `price + price * 8.25%` works. It does not, and cannot under the
current rules. A percent literal is exactly two scales wider than the
percentage written (`8.25%` == 0.0825 == `Decimal<4>`), while `price` is
`Decimal<2>` — and Burxt requires `Decimal * Decimal` operands to have the
SAME scale. So percent-of-money, the single most canonical money operation,
falls outside the arithmetic rules as written.

What works today (shipped in v0.0.18) is percent-of-money at a matching scale:

```burxt
let price: Decimal<4, RoundHalfEven> = 19.9900;
let pct:   Decimal<4, RoundHalfEven> = 8.25%;
let tax:   Decimal<4, RoundHalfEven> = price * pct;   // 1.6492
```

**The decision:** should `*` allow MIXED scales when a rounding contract says
how to land? Arguments both ways, honestly:

- **For.** Money × rate is the normal shape of the operation, and the two
  operands genuinely have different natural scales. `Decimal<2,R> *
  Decimal<4>` → round the exact scale-6 product to 2 by R. Well-defined, and
  the i128 intermediates from v0.0.11 already make it exact.
- **Against.** "Scales must match" is a thesis rule that catches real bugs,
  and relaxing it for `*` means `+` and `*` no longer share one rule — a
  reader can no longer assume mixed scales are always refused.
- **A middle reading:** matching scales is right for `+`/`-` (adding
  different-precision money IS suspicious) but wrong for `*` (scaling by a
  rate is not). That asymmetry may be principled rather than inconsistent.

This is deliberately NOT decided here, because it changes a core thesis rule
and is exactly the kind of call that should not arrive as a side effect of a
literal form — the same reasoning as Amendment 2. Until it is decided, the
narrowing error names the fix (widen the binding) rather than failing
mysteriously.

## 0. What "signature grammar" means
The features that make Burxt code read like a description of the problem — money and
units as literals, contracts as part of a function's signature, string interpolation.
These are what make a demo land ("oh, that's nice") without making every line exotic.

## 1. Money & unit literals
### 1a. Money
- `$19.99` is a literal of type `Decimal<2>`. The `$` prefix means "this is money,
  scale 2," so no type annotation is needed: `let price = $19.99;` infers `Decimal<2>`.
- `$0.00`, `$1000.50`, etc. Scale is always 2 for `$` (the currency-minor-unit norm).
- Semantically identical to writing `Decimal<2>` — it is sugar over the existing
  exact-decimal type, NOT a new type. All the existing decimal rules apply unchanged.

### 1b. Percent
- `8.25%` is a literal meaning 8.25 percent, represented exactly (not as a float, not
  as `8.25/100`). Internally a `Decimal` with the right scale such that
  `price + price * 8.25%` is exact. Define the representation so `price * 8.25%`
  yields the correct scaled result and requires a rounding contract if scales demand
  it (reuse A1 rounding rules — percent-of-money is decimal*decimal).

### 1c. Units (the general case — NOT just money)
- `5.km`, `30.min`, `10.kg`, `100.usd`, `3.days` — a numeric literal with a unit
  suffix carries the unit in its *type*.
- **Illegal mixing is a compile error.** `price + 5.km` (money + distance) → error.
  `5.usd + 3.eur` (different currencies) → error, "cannot add USD and EUR; convert
  explicitly." `5.km + 30.min` → error.
- **Legal same-unit ops** proceed: `5.km + 3.km = 8.km`.
- Units are a small, FIXED, built-in set in A4.7 (currency codes, a few SI units).
  NOT user-definable yet — that is deferred (§5).

## 2. Contracts as first-class grammar (the verification differentiator)
`requires` / `ensures` are KEYWORDS, part of the function signature, not attributes.

```burxt
fn withdraw(acct: mut Account, amount: Decimal<2>)
    requires amount > $0.00
    ensures  acct.balance >= $0.00
{
    acct.balance = acct.balance - amount;
}
```

- `requires <bool-expr>`: a precondition. Checked at the call site.
- `ensures <bool-expr>`: a postcondition. Checked at return. `ensures` may reference
  the return value (syntax: a keyword like `result` naming it) and the final state.
- **Enforcement tier for A4.7** (be honest about scope): compile-time *static*
  verification of arbitrary contracts is the full verification thesis and is HARD
  (SMT-solver territory). A4.7 delivers the **grammar and runtime-checked enforcement**:
  contracts parse, typecheck (must be `Bool`), and lower to runtime guards that trap
  with a clear message on violation. Static/compile-time proving of contracts is a
  *later* milestone (refinement types / verification, its own phase). This staging is
  the honest version: ship the syntax and runtime checks now; prove them at compile
  time later. Mark clearly in DESIGN.md so "contracts" is not mistaken for "verified
  at compile time" yet.

## 3. String interpolation
- `"Account of {owner}: {balance}"` — `{expr}` splices a value's display form.
- Default over concatenation and printf-juggling.
- Interpolated expressions are typechecked; only displayable types allowed (Int,
  Decimal, Bool, String initially). Reuses the exact-decimal printing from codegen.

## 4. Pipelines (proven, safe eloquence)
- `x |> f |> g` means `g(f(x))` — left-to-right instead of inside-out.
- `|> f(a)` means `f(x, a)` (piped value is the first argument).
- Pure sugar: lowers to ordinary calls, zero runtime cost, no new semantics.
- Deferred until there are enough functions/collections to make it useful (fast
  follow, not deep defer). Include only if it composes cleanly with what exists.

## 5. What A4.7 must NOT do
- **NO user-defined units.** Fixed built-in set only. User-definable units/dimensional
  analysis is a real feature with real complexity (dimension algebra). Trigger: a
  required program needs a domain unit outside the built-in set.
- **NO compile-time contract proving.** Runtime-checked only in A4.7 (§2). Static
  verification is the refinement-types phase.
- **NO arbitrary currency conversion built in.** `5.usd + 3.eur` errors; it does not
  silently convert. Conversion requires explicit, rate-supplying code. (No hidden FX.)
- **NO format-spec mini-language** in interpolation (`{x:.2f}` etc.). Values print in
  their canonical exact form; formatting DSLs are a later, separate concern.
- **NO operator overloading** to make units work — units are handled in typeck by the
  built-in rules, not by user `impl`s. (Consistent with A4.6's no-operator-traits.)

## 6. Deliverables
1. Money `$` literal → `Decimal<2>` (lexer, typeck inference).
2. Percent literal, exact representation, correct in arithmetic.
3. Unit literals with the fixed built-in set; illegal-mixing compile errors.
4. `requires`/`ensures` keywords; Bool-typed; runtime-guard lowering with clear traps.
5. String interpolation `{expr}`, typechecked, exact display.
6. Pipeline `|>` sugar (if it composes cleanly; else defer to fast-follow).
7. Tests, rejection-weighted:
   - `let price = $19.99` infers Decimal<2>; `price + price*8.25%` exact;
   - `5.km + 3.km` works; **rejections**: money+distance, USD+EUR, different units;
   - a violated `requires`/`ensures` traps with the right message; a satisfied one
     is transparent;
   - interpolation prints exact decimal; **rejection**: interpolating a non-display type.
